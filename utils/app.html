CrashDefNet app

A graph neural network learns from finite element simulations how a structure deforms, then predicts this on its own, time step by time step, for simulations it has never seen. Follow the five steps for a first run in minutes, or go straight to any step to work with your own datasets, configurations, models and predictions.

    1 · Raw data

    A first run needs no data of your own: the model learns from DeepMind's deforming_plate dataset, finite element simulations in which an actuator presses into a plate and deforms it. Every plate has its own outline, holes and mesh, and the actuators differ in shape and in where they press. A simulation runs 400 time steps: the actuator presses in until step 360, then pulls back, and the plate springs back.

    Six of the plates at their deepest indentation, time step 360. Colour: von Mises stress, from light blue (0) to dark blue (300 kPa and above); grey: the actuator.

    This needs the test file of the dataset, 100 simulated plates (0.8 GB), downloaded once into . With datasets of your own you can skip this step.

    The data comes under DeepMind's terms, not CrashDefNet's license (MeshGraphNets release).

    2 · Dataset

    Each simulation becomes a sequence of graphs: the nodes of the finite element mesh, connected along the mesh and where parts touch. Training simulations teach the model, check simulations pick its best training epoch, and the unseen simulations are what it predicts at the end.

    Use a dataset

    Every export in data/ appears here, also of your own simulations: LS-DYNA runs and VTK time series are exported on the command line (guide).

    Or create a new one from deforming_plate

    3 · Training

    From one time step the model learns to predict the next: how each node moves and which stress it carries. After every epoch it predicts the check simulations on its own, and the epoch that does this best is kept.

    4 · Prediction

    The trained model predicts the unseen simulations from the first time step to the last, each step from its own previous prediction, and compares them with the ground truth. Then the app renders the first simulation as an animation, so that step 5 can show it right away.
